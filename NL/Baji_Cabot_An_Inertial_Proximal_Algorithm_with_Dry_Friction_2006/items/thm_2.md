## Statement explanation
Let $H$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space. The theorem is a finite-termination result for the algorithm represented by `AlgorithmA`. It involves two set-valued operators $A,B:H\rightrightarrows H$, a real-valued strongly convex function encoded as an extended-real function $f:H\to\overline{\mathbb R}$, and a sequence $x:\mathbb N\to H$.

The assumptions are as follows. The operator $A$ is maximal monotone, has domain all of $H$, and satisfies the nondegeneracy condition $0\in \mathrm{int} A(0)$. The constants satisfy $\eta>0$, $\alpha\ge0$, and the operator $A$ has the local linear-growth/excess estimate

$$
\mathrm{excess}(A x,A(0))\le \alpha\|x\|
$$

whenever $\|x\|\le\eta$. The function $f$ is finite-valued in the sense that $f(x)$ is neither $\top$ nor $\bot$ for every $x$, its real-valued version $x\mapsto (f(x))_{\mathbb R}$ is $C^1$ (through the hypothesis `IsC11`, whose first component is used as $C^1$-regularity), it is bounded below by some real constant, and it is $M$-strongly convex. The operator $B$ is exactly the gradient operator of that real-valued function:

$$
B(x)=\{\nabla ((f(\cdot))_{\mathbb R})(x)\}
$$

on all of $H$. The step-size sequence is constant: $\lambda_n=\lambda$ for some $\lambda>0$. The sequence $x$ is generated by Algorithm A and converges to a point $x_\infty$. Finally, the set $A(0)$ is smooth at the point $-\nabla ((f(\cdot))_{\mathbb R})(x_\infty)$, and the key quantitative hypothesis is

$$
\alpha^2<M.
$$

The conclusion is that the sequence $x$ is eventually constant:

$$
\exists N,\ \forall n\ge N,\quad x_n=x_N.
$$

So the theorem says that if the local linear-growth constant $\alpha$ is strictly smaller than the square root of the strong-convexity modulus $M$, then a convergent trajectory of this algorithm cannot remain nonstationary forever: after finitely many steps all iterates are equal. In other words, the algorithm terminates in finite time.

## Proof explanation
The proof is by contradiction. It assumes that the sequence is not eventually constant, i.e.

$$
\neg\exists N,\ \forall n\ge N,\quad x_n=x_N,
$$

and calls this hypothesis `hnot_finite`. The goal is to derive a contradiction. From the strong-convexity hypothesis the proof first extracts $M>0$: the command `have hMpos : 0 < M := hf_strongConv.1` uses the positivity component carried by `IsStronglyConvex`.

Next, the proof invokes `thm41_rate_interval_nonempty`. With the hypotheses $\alpha\ge0$, $M>0$, $\lambda>0$, and $\alpha^2<M$, that lemma produces two real rates $q_2,q_1$ such that

$$
0<q_2,\qquad q_2<q_1,\qquad q_1<1,
$$

and

$$
q_1<\frac{1}{\alpha\lambda+1},\qquad
q_2>\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+M\lambda}.
$$

Thus $q_1$ is a rate lying below the lower-rate threshold, while $q_2$ lies above the upper-rate threshold. The proof then records $q_1>0$ and $q_2<1$, both by transitivity from $0<q_2<q_1<1$.

The next major step applies `thm_4_1_lower_rate_of_not_finite`. Under all the standing assumptions and under the contradiction hypothesis that the sequence is not eventually constant, that theorem states that for every $q_1$ with $0<q_1<1$ and $q_1<1/(\alpha\lambda+1)$, there exist a positive constant $C_1$ and an index $n_{\rm lower}$ such that for all $n\ge n_{\rm lower}$,

$$
\|x_n-x_\infty\|\ge C_1 q_1^{\,n}.
$$

Specializing to the previously chosen $q_1$ gives a positive constant $C_1$ and a lower geometric bound for the distances from $x_\infty$.

Similarly, the proof applies `thm_4_1_tail_rate_of_not_finite`. Under the same hypotheses, that theorem says that for every $q_2$ with $0<q_2<1$ and $q_2>\alpha/(\alpha+M\lambda)$, there exist a positive constant $C_2$ and an index $n_{\rm tail}$ such that for all $n\ge n_{\rm tail}$,

$$
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k}-x_\infty\|\le C_2 q_2^{\,n}.
$$

Specializing to the chosen $q_2$ gives a positive constant $C_2$ and an upper geometric bound on the whole tail series of distances.

At this point the proof has a lower bound on the individual distances and an upper bound on the tail sums. To combine them, it must convert the tail upper bound into an eventual pointwise upper bound

$$
\|x_n-x_\infty\|\le C_2 q_2^{\,n}.
$$

This is done in the block proving `hPointUpper`. The proof first uses `thm41_smoothAt_unit_normal`. Since $A(0)$ is smooth at $-\nabla ((f(\cdot))_{\mathbb R})(x_\infty)$, that lemma gives a unit vector $u\in H$, $\|u\|=1$, such that the normal cone is exactly the ray generated by $u$:

$$
\mathrm{normalCone}\bigl(A(0),-\nabla ((f(\cdot))_{\mathbb R})(x_\infty)\bigr)
=\{v\mid \exists t\ge0,\ v=t u\}.
$$

This unit normal is needed later for the one-dimensional scalar-product estimate.

The proof defines

$$
D:=\mathrm{stationaryIndices}(x)
$$

and

$$
R_n:=\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k+1}-x_{n+k}\|.
$$

So $R_n$ is the tail sum of successive increments after time $n$. The set $D$ records the indices where the sequence does not move; it is used as an input to the norm-equivalence theorem.

Next, the proof obtains a convexity inequality for the extended-real-valued $f$. It invokes `thm41_ereal_convex_of_strongConvex`. That lemma says that if $f$ is finite-valued and its real-valued version is $M$-strongly convex, then for all $x,y\in H$ and all $a,b\ge0$ with $a+b=1$,

$$
f(a x+b y)\le (a:\overline{\mathbb R})f(x)+(b:\overline{\mathbb R})f(y).
$$

This is the extended-real form of convexity needed by `prop_4_1_norm_equiv`.

The proof also checks that the constant step-size sequence satisfies the positivity and monotonicity hypotheses used by that norm-equivalence theorem. Since $\lambda_n=\lambda$ and $\lambda>0$, each $\lambda_n$ is positive; and since all $\lambda_n$ are equal to $\lambda$, the sequence is nondecreasing. The corresponding Lean steps use `rw [hlam_seq n]` to replace $\lambda_n$ by $\lambda$, after which the positivity and reflexivity goals are immediate.

Then the proof applies `prop_4_1_norm_equiv`. Under the standing assumptions, the extended convexity inequality, the $C^1$ regularity, the lower bound on $f$, the gradient representation of $B$, the fixed step size, the convergence $x_n\to x_\infty$, the smoothness condition, the unit normal $u$, and the contradiction hypothesis, that theorem gives the asymptotic equivalence

$$
\bigl(n\mapsto \|x_n-x_\infty\|\bigr)\sim_{\rm atTop} R.
$$

Thus the distances from $x_\infty$ and the increment-tail sums $R_n$ have the same asymptotic size.

The proof now proves that $R$ is summable. First, $R_n\ge0$ for every $n$, because it is a sum of nonnegative norms. Second, since $x_n\to x_\infty$, the distances satisfy

$$
\|x_n-x_\infty\|\to0.
$$

The proof obtains this by showing $x_n-x_\infty\to0$ and then composing with the continuous norm map. Because $R$ is asymptotically equivalent to the distance sequence, this implies

$$
R_n\to0.
$$

Next, the proof uses `thm41_eventual_distance_pos_of_not_finite`. That lemma states that under the same assumptions and the non-eventual-constancy hypothesis, there is $N_{\rm pos}$ such that for all $n\ge N_{\rm pos}$,

$$
0<\|x_n-x_\infty\|.
$$

Thus the distances are eventually positive. Since $R$ is asymptotically equivalent to the distances, $R_n$ is also eventually positive. So the proof has

$$
\forall^{\rm eventually} n,\quad 0<R_n.
$$

For such $n$, $R_n$ is a positive sum of the increment norms:

$$
0<\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k+1}-x_{n+k}\|.
$$

The proof then invokes `thm41_summable_of_tsum_pos`, which says that if a real series has positive total sum, then the underlying sequence is summable. Consequently, for all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k+1}-x_{n+k}\|
$$

is a convergent series, i.e. the increment sequence after $n$ is summable.

Using this eventual summability, the proof applies `thm41_increment_tail_tsum_eq_head_add_tail`. That lemma says that if the increment sequence from $n$ is summable, then

$$
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k+1}-x_{n+k}\|
=\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|
+\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+1+k+1}-x_{n+1+k}\|.
$$

In the notation of the proof, this becomes eventually

$$
R_n=\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|+R_{n+1}.
$$

Because the first term is nonnegative, this immediately gives the eventual monotonicity

$$
R_{n+1}\le R_n.
$$

The proof then defines

$$
\mathrm{inc}_n:=\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|
$$

and

$$
p_n:=\lambda^{-2}\langle x_{n+1}-x_n,u\rangle.
$$

From the eventual summability of the increment tails, it obtains that the full increment sequence $\mathrm{inc}$ is summable. This uses the standard shift equivalence for summability of tails: if a tail $\mathrm{inc}_{N+k}$ is summable, then the original sequence $\mathrm{inc}$ is summable.

Now the proof uses `thm41_eventual_step_ineq` with the choice $\mu=M/2$. Since $M>0$, indeed $M/2<M$. The lemma produces an index $N_{\rm step}$ such that for all $n\ge N_{\rm step}$,

$$
-\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}\|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}\|
+\frac{M}{2}
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+2+k+1}-x_{n+2+k}\|
\le
\lambda^{-2}\langle x_{n+2}-x_{n+1},u\rangle
-\lambda^{-2}\langle x_{n+1}-x_n,u\rangle.
$$

In the proof’s abbreviations this is exactly

$$
-\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}\mathrm{inc}_{n+1}
+\frac{M}{2}R_{n+2}
\le
p_{n+1}-p_n.
$$

This is a discrete inequality relating a damped increment term and the future increment tail to the difference of scalar products with the unit normal.

The proof also needs a bound on the size of $p_n$. It proves

$$
|p_n|\le \lambda^{-2}\mathrm{inc}_n.
$$

Indeed, with $d=x_{n+1}-x_n$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$
|\langle d,u\rangle|\le \|d\|\,\|u\|=\|d\|,
$$

because $\|u\|=1$. Multiplying by the nonnegative constant $\lambda^{-2}$ gives the displayed bound. The Lean proof uses a `calc` block: absolute value of a scalar multiple, Cauchy–Schwarz, and then the equality $\|u\|=1$.

Now all hypotheses of `thm41_summable_R_of_step_ineq` are available. That lemma states: if $\mathrm{inc}_n\ge0$, $R_n\ge0$, $\mathrm{inc}$ is summable, $m>0$, $a\ge0$, $\beta\ge0$, $|p_n|\le \beta \mathrm{inc}_n$, and eventually

$$
-a\,\mathrm{inc}_{n+1}+m R_{n+2}\le p_{n+1}-p_n,
$$

then $R$ is summable. The proof applies it with

$$
a=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda},\qquad m=\frac{M}{2},\qquad \beta=\lambda^{-2}.
$$

The required nonnegativity of $a$ follows from $\alpha\ge0$ and $\lambda>0$; the positivity of $m$ follows from $M>0$; and the nonnegativity of $\beta$ follows from $\lambda\ne0$ and is handled by `positivity`. Therefore

$$
R
$$

is summable.

Once $R$ is summable, the proof applies `thm41_tail_summable_of_equiv_summable`. Since $R$ is summable and the distance sequence is asymptotically equivalent to $R$, that lemma gives an index after which every tail of the distance sequence is summable:

$$
\exists n_0,\ \forall n\ge n_0,\quad
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k}-x_\infty\|
\text{ is summable}.
$$

This is `hTailSummable`.

Now the tail upper estimate from `thm_4_1_tail_rate_of_not_finite` can be converted into a pointwise upper estimate. The proof uses `thm41_pointwise_upper_of_tail_tsum_upper`. That lemma says that if, for all sufficiently large $n$, the tail sums are summable and satisfy

$$
\sum_{k\ge0}\|x_{n+k}-x_\infty\|\le C q^n,
$$

then for all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
\|x_n-x_\infty\|\le C q^n.
$$

The reason is simply that the first term of the tail is the distance itself and all other terms are nonnegative, so the whole tail is at least that first term. Applying this with $C=C_2$ and $q=q_2$ gives the desired eventual pointwise upper bound

$$
\|x_n-x_\infty\|\le C_2 q_2^{\,n}.
$$

At this point the proof has both an eventual lower bound and an eventual upper bound. Taking $n_0=\max(n_{\rm lower},n_{\rm upper})$, it obtains for all $n\ge n_0$,

$$
C_1 q_1^{\,n}\le \|x_n-x_\infty\|\le C_2 q_2^{\,n}.
$$

But $q_2<q_1$ and $q_2>0$. Therefore the lower geometric sequence with base $q_1$ eventually dominates the upper geometric sequence with base $q_2$: since $q_1/q_2>1$,

$$
(q_1/q_2)^n\to\infty,
$$

so for sufficiently large $n$,

$$
C_1 q_1^{\,n}>C_2 q_2^{\,n},
$$

which contradicts the chain of inequalities above.

This final contradiction is exactly the content of `thm41_eventual_geometric_bounds_incompatible`: if $C_1>0$, $q_2>0$, $q_2<q_1$, and eventually

$$
C_1 q_1^{\,n}\le a_n\le C_2 q_2^{\,n},
$$

then `False`. The proof applies this lemma with

$$
a_n=\|x_n-x_\infty\|,
$$

using the lower and upper estimates just established. Since this produces a contradiction, the assumption that the sequence is not eventually constant is false. Hence the original conclusion holds: there exists $N$ such that for all $n\ge N$,

$$
x_n=x_N.
$$

The Lean commands in the proof correspond directly to these mathematical steps: `by_contra` begins the contradiction argument; `rcases` unpacks existential and conjunction hypotheses; `rw` replaces $\lambda_n$ by $\lambda$; `filter_upwards` manipulates “eventually” statements; `positivity` proves nonnegativity of $\lambda^{-2}$; `nlinarith` proves elementary numeric inequalities such as $M/2>0$ and $\alpha/\lambda\ge0$; `calc` chains the Cauchy–Schwarz estimate; and `exact` supplies the final incompatible-geometric-bounds lemma, yielding the contradiction.
